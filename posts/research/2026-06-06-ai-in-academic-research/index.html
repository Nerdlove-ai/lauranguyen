What happens when AI writes accounting research — and when it hallucinates results

June 6, 2026

Source: Ciconte, Rozario & Urcan (2026) — “Using AI to Identify Exogenous Shocks and Conduct Archival Accounting Research”

There is a particular kind of panic that only research can create.

You think you have an idea.

Then you realise you need a clean research design, a valid shock, the right dataset, a defensible method, and ideally a small miracle.

So of course the idea of using AI as a research assistant is tempting.

Ask it to find exogenous shocks. Ask it to draft the paper. Ask it to explain the mechanism. Maybe ask it to also reassure you that doing a doctorate was a good life choice 😭

This paper tested that temptation in a very direct way.

The researchers used AI to identify regulatory shocks in securities rules, test whether those shocks affected voluntary disclosure, and then draft academic papers from the findings. They tried this with U.S. regulations and then with non-U.S. regulations.

The U.S. case was encouraging.

AI was reasonably useful at identifying possible shocks and giving the researchers a starting point. The draft paper was not ready for a top accounting journal, which is a polite academic way of saying “please do not submit this unless you enjoy rejection.” But it saved time. It helped structure the work. It gave the researchers something to interrogate.

That is the good version of AI in research.

Not genius.

Not independent scholar.

More like a fast assistant who gives you a messy first map, and then you still have to check whether the roads exist.

The non-U.S. case was where things became much less cute.

AI produced professional-looking papers with weak or unsupported economic arguments. The papers sounded like research. They had the structure. They had the tone. They looked serious.

But the results did not hold up.

That is the dangerous part.

Bad research used to look bad more often. Messy logic, awkward structure, missing pieces. Now AI can make weak work look polished. It can wrap uncertainty in confident academic language and make a fragile claim sound like it has been through peer review, therapy, and a full skincare routine.

This is what worries me most.

AI output often looks equally fluent whether it is helping, guessing, or completely making things up. The quality difference is not obvious from the writing style. You have to inspect the reasoning, the data, and the identification strategy.

In research, that matters a lot.

Because the hard part is not just writing the paper. The hard part is knowing whether the evidence actually supports the claim.

AI can help when the task is familiar, the prior literature is rich, and errors are easy to check. It can be useful for brainstorming, summarising, drafting, and finding starting points.

But when the work requires deep domain judgment or causal reasoning, AI needs a very awake human next to it.

Preferably one who has had coffee.

My takeaway is not “do not use AI for research.”

I use AI all the time. Obviously. My sleep schedule has receipts.

The takeaway is that AI should make researchers faster, not less responsible.

If anything, it raises the standard for human judgment. Because now the machine can produce a paper-shaped object very quickly.

The researcher still has to decide whether it is actually research.
