The obfuscation hypothesis — why bad news comes wrapped in difficult prose

June 6, 2026

Source: Li (2008) — “Annual Report Readability, Current Earnings, and Earnings Persistence”

This paper is a foundational piece that I keep returning to because it establishes something important and counterintuitive with a simple observation: the annual reports of firms with lower earnings are harder to read.

The author measures readability using the Fog index from computational linguistics — a formula that estimates the years of education required to understand a piece of text, based on sentence length and the proportion of complex words. Applied to 10-K filings across a large sample, the pattern is consistent: firms with poor earnings produce less readable filings. Firms with easier-to-read reports have more persistent positive earnings.

The interpretation is the obfuscation hypothesis: when performance is bad, management has an incentive to make the bad news harder to extract. Longer documents, more complex sentences, more technical language — all of these increase the cognitive cost of reading the filing, which can slow or dilute the market’s reaction to negative information. If fewer investors successfully decode the bad news, the stock price falls less than it should.

What makes this finding particularly interesting is the persistence finding. Firms with more readable reports have earnings that persist — that is, current earnings are a better predictor of future earnings. This suggests that readability correlates with actual business clarity, not just disclosure quality. Companies whose businesses are genuinely straightforward produce straightforward disclosures. Companies with complex, struggling, or deteriorating businesses produce complex ones.

The market inefficiency angle: if the obfuscation strategy works — if less readable filings genuinely slow price adjustment to bad news — then investors who are better at extracting information from difficult text should systematically outperform. And conversely, companies that rely on complexity to mask poor results are eventually exposed when the earnings persist in the wrong direction.

This paper predates most modern NLP applications in finance, but it provides the conceptual foundation for a large body of subsequent work. Readability, sentiment, tone, length — these aren’t just stylistic features of disclosure. They’re strategic variables with measurable market consequences.
